## Start with an evidence-based picture

A country-level economic overview should separate facts from explanations. Begin with indicators such as output, employment, trade, remittance income, poverty, and sectoral composition. Then ask what resources, institutions, and constraints help explain those indicators.

Nepal’s economic structure includes agriculture, services, tourism, trade, remittances, and a developing industrial base. Their relative importance changes over time, so current data should be checked before making a strong claim.

## Natural resources

Water, forests, land, biodiversity, and minerals can support production, energy, livelihoods, and public revenue. Their economic value depends on access, technology, institutions, sustainability, and the opportunity cost of alternative uses.

Resource abundance does not automatically create development. Poor management, environmental damage, weak infrastructure, and unequal access can reduce long-term benefits. Sustainable use means considering both current needs and future productive capacity.

## Agriculture

Agriculture contributes to food security, employment, raw materials, and rural livelihoods. Productivity can be affected by land fragmentation, irrigation, technology, access to inputs, finance, markets, storage, and transport. Modernisation and commercialisation should therefore be analysed as connected changes in production and market access.

For a local observation, record the crop or product, inputs used, labour, market, transport, risks, and one possible productivity improvement.

## Industry and tourism

Industry adds value by transforming raw materials into goods and services. Its development depends on energy, skills, finance, technology, infrastructure, market size, and policy. Tourism can generate employment and foreign exchange, but it also depends on accessibility, service quality, safety, seasonality, and environmental management.

A strong answer identifies both potential and constraints rather than listing advantages only.

## Transport and communication

Transport connects producers to markets and workers to employment. Communication lowers information costs and helps households, firms, and governments coordinate decisions. Weak connectivity can reduce productivity even when natural or human resources are available.
