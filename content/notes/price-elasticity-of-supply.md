## 1. What is Price Elasticity of Supply?

**Price Elasticity of Supply ($E_s$)** measures the degree of responsiveness or sensitivity of the quantity supplied of a commodity to a change in its **selling price**, holding other factors constant (*ceteris paribus*).

$$E_s = \dfrac{\% \text{ Change in Quantity Supplied}}{\% \text{ Change in Price}} = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \dfrac{P_1}{Q_1}$$

* $P_1$ = Initial price, $P_2$ = New price, $\Delta P = P_2 - P_1$
* $Q_1$ = Initial quantity supplied, $Q_2$ = New quantity supplied, $\Delta Q = Q_2 - Q_1$
* Because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction (Law of Supply), the elasticity of supply coefficient is always a **positive number** ($E_s \ge 0$).

## 2. The Five Degrees of Elasticity of Supply

Economists classify the elasticity of supply into **five distinct ranges**:

![Five Degrees of Price Elasticity of Supply](/images/uploads/five-degrees-supply-elasticity.svg)

### 1. Perfectly Inelastic Supply ($E_s = 0$)
* **Concept:** The quantity supplied remains completely fixed and unchanged regardless of how high or low the market price rises ($\% \Delta Q_s = 0$).
* **Curve Shape:** A **vertical straight line** parallel to the price ($Y$) axis.
* **Real-World Examples:** Rare historic art (e.g., Leonardo da Vinci's *Mona Lisa*), unique land plots, and fresh fish catch in the immediate market period.

### 2. Relatively Inelastic Supply ($E_s < 1$)
* **Concept:** The percentage change in quantity supplied is **smaller than** the percentage change in price ($\% \Delta Q_s < \% \Delta P$).
* **Curve Shape:** A **steep upward-sloping curve** that intersects the positive quantity ($X$) axis.
* **Real-World Examples:** Agricultural crops (wheat, apples, coffee) in the short run where output cannot be expanded quickly before harvest season.

### 3. Unitary Elastic Supply ($E_s = 1$)
* **Concept:** The percentage change in quantity supplied is **exactly equal** to the percentage change in price ($\% \Delta Q_s = \% \Delta P$).
* **Curve Shape:** A straight-line supply curve that **passes through the origin $(0, 0)$**.
* **Real-World Examples:** Standard manufacturing assembly items where production can scale in exact proportion to price increases.

### 4. Relatively Elastic Supply ($E_s > 1$)
* **Concept:** The percentage change in quantity supplied is **greater than** the percentage change in price ($\% \Delta Q_s > \% \Delta P$).
* **Curve Shape:** A **flatter upward-sloping curve** that intersects the positive price ($Y$) axis.
* **Real-World Examples:** Manufactured durable goods (furniture, consumer electronics, textiles) where factories have spare capacity and large stored inventories.

### 5. Perfectly Elastic Supply ($E_s = \infty$)
* **Concept:** Sellers are willing to supply an infinite quantity at a specific price level ($P$), but if the price falls even slightly, supply drops to zero.
* **Curve Shape:** A **horizontal straight line** parallel to the quantity ($X$) axis.
* **Real-World Examples:** Standardized digital software licenses or commoditized goods in purely competitive wholesale auctions.

## 4. Key Factors Determining Elasticity of Supply

1. **Time Horizon for Production Adjustment:**
   * **Market Period (Very Short Run):** Supply is almost completely fixed ($E_s \approx 0$).
   * **Short Run:** Supply is relatively inelastic ($E_s < 1$) as only variable inputs can be added.
   * **Long Run:** Supply is highly elastic ($E_s > 1$) because firms can build new factories, hire workers, and expand plant capacity.
2. **Nature of the Good (Perishable vs. Durable):**
   * Perishable items (fresh milk, vegetables) have inelastic supply because they cannot be stored for long periods.
   * Durable goods (steel, dry timber, packaged goods) have elastic supply because producers can store inventory and release it when prices rise.
3. **Availability of Spare Capacity and Stock:**
   * Factories operating at 60% capacity can rapidly expand output when prices rise ($E_s > 1$).
   * Factories running at 100% capacity cannot quickly expand output ($E_s < 1$).
4. **Mobility and Flexibility of Factors of Production:**
   * If workers and machinery can be easily shifted from producing one good to another (e.g., from making cotton shirts to trousers), supply is highly elastic.

## 5. Review & Exam Practice Questions

### Very Short Questions (1-2 Marks):
1. **What is the value of price elasticity of supply for a vertical supply curve?**  
   *Answer:* Zero ($E_s = 0$, Perfectly Inelastic Supply).
2. **Under what price-quantity condition is supply called elastic?**  
   *Answer:* When percentage change in quantity supplied exceeds percentage change in price ($E_s > 1$).
3. **What is the geometric property of a unitary elastic supply curve?**  
   *Answer:* It is a straight line passing through the origin ($0, 0$).

### Short Questions (3-5 Marks):
1. Define price elasticity of supply and explain its five degrees with diagrams.
2. Explain four major factors that determine the price elasticity of supply of a product.
