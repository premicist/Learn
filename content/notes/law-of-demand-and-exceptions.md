## 1. Statement of the Law of Demand

The **Law of Demand** is one of the most fundamental principles of microeconomics. Formulated systematically by **Alfred Marshall** in *Principles of Economics* (1890), it states:

> *"Other things being equal (ceteris paribus), the quantity demanded of a commodity increases with a fall in its price, and diminishes with a rise in its price."*

In mathematical terms, there is an **inverse (negative) relationship** between the price of a good and the quantity demanded:

$$\text{Demand Function: } Q_d = f(P_x) \quad \text{where } \dfrac{dQ_d}{dP_x} < 0$$

$$P_x \uparrow \implies Q_d \downarrow \quad \text{and} \quad P_x \downarrow \implies Q_d \uparrow$$

---

## 2. Assumptions of the Law of Demand (*Ceteris Paribus*)

The Law of Demand holds true only when all other non-price factors influencing demand remain constant (*Ceteris Paribus*):

1. **Consumer income ($Y$) remains constant:** No increase or decrease in money income.
2. **Prices of related goods ($P_r$) remain unchanged:** Prices of substitutes and complements stay constant.
3. **Consumer tastes, habits, and preferences ($T$) remain unchanged.**
4. **No future price expectations ($E$):** Buyers do not anticipate future price hikes or shortages.
5. **Size and demographic composition of the population ($N$) remain constant.**
6. **Government policy and indirect taxes remain stable.**

## 3. Demand Schedule and the Demand Curve

### A. Individual Demand Schedule
A **demand schedule** is a numerical table showing the different quantities of a commodity that a consumer is willing to purchase at various price levels during a specific time period.

| Price per Unit (Rs. P) | Quantity Demanded (Units Q) | Consumer Market Response |
| :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **Rs. 50** | 10 | Highest price; minimum quantity demanded |
| **Rs. 40** | 20 | Price drops by Rs. 10; demand expands to 20 units |
| **Rs. 30** | 30 | Moderate equilibrium price level |
| **Rs. 20** | 40 | Low price attracts budget-conscious purchases |
| **Rs. 10** | 50 | Lowest price; maximum quantity demanded (Expansion) |

### B. The Demand Curve
Plotting price on the vertical axis ($Y$-axis) and quantity demanded on the horizontal axis ($X$-axis) yields the **Demand Curve ($DD$)**:

## 4. Why Does the Demand Curve Slope Downwards?

Economists identify **five major reasons** why consumers buy more at lower prices:

![law of demand and exceptions diagram 1](/flowcharts/introduction-to-economics/law-of-demand-and-exceptions-diagram-1.svg)

1. **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (DMU):**  
   According to Alfred Marshall, as a consumer consumes more units of a good, the marginal utility ($MU$) derived from each additional unit continuously declines. A rational consumer will only buy additional units if the price falls ($P_x = \dfrac{MU_x}{MU_m}$).
2. **Income Effect:**  
   When the price of a commodity falls, the consumer's **real income (purchasing power)** increases, allowing them to buy more units of the good with the same budget.
3. **Substitution Effect:**  
   When the price of Good $X$ falls while the prices of substitute goods remain unchanged, Good $X$ becomes relatively cheaper, encouraging buyers to substitute $X$ in place of other goods.
4. **New Buyers Entering the Market:**  
   When the price drops, lower-income consumers who could not previously afford the product enter the market as new buyers.
5. **Multiple Alternative Uses:**  
   Goods like milk or electricity have multiple uses. At high prices, they are used only for essential needs. When prices fall, they are used for secondary purposes (e.g., milk for cheese, butter, and sweets).

## 5. Exceptions to the Law of Demand (Upward-Sloping Demand Curve)

Under certain exceptional circumstances, the Law of Demand does not operate. In these cases, a rise in price leads to an increase in quantity demanded ($P \uparrow \implies Q_d \uparrow$), creating an **upward-sloping demand curve**:

| Exception Category | Economic Cause / Mechanism | Real-World & Business Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Giffen Goods** | Extreme inferior staple goods where a strong negative income effect overpowers the substitution effect. | Coarse grains (millet, bajra) or staple potatoes among low-income households. |
| **2. Veblen Goods (Conspicuous Consumption)** | Prestige goods purchased for social status or snob appeal; higher price signals exclusivity (Thorstein Veblen). | Luxury sports cars, designer watches, vintage wines, and presidential hotel suites. |
| **3. Speculative Goods** | When prices rise, buyers expect even higher future prices and purchase more today to avoid future shortages. | Real estate property, gold, stock market shares, and wedding banquet bookings during peak season. |
| **4. Consumer Ignorance (Price-Quality Illusion)** | Buyers mistakenly equate higher prices with superior product quality and avoid cheaper alternatives. | Branded designer cosmetics vs. identical generic alternatives; premium restaurant items. |
| **5. Life-Saving Necessities & Emergencies** | Essential survival goods must be purchased regardless of escalating prices during crises. | Emergency medicines, oxygen cylinders, hospital beds, and basic food supplies during natural disasters. |

## 6. Review & Exam Practice Questions

### Very Short Questions (1-2 Marks):
1. **What is the slope of a standard demand curve?**  
   *Answer:* Negative (slopes downward from left to right).
2. **What is a Giffen good?**  
   *Answer:* A special category of inferior staple good for which demand increases when price rises ($P \uparrow \implies Q_d \uparrow$).
3. **What is the difference between Normal Goods and Inferior Goods?**  
   *Answer:* Demand for normal goods increases as income rises; demand for inferior goods decreases as income rises.

### Short Questions (3-5 Marks):
1. State the Law of Demand and list four of its core assumptions (*ceteris paribus*).
2. Explain the five reasons why the demand curve slopes downward from left to right.
3. Discuss the main exceptions to the Law of Demand with examples.
