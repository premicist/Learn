## Why national income is measured

National income accounting records the value of economic activity over a period. It helps describe production, income, expenditure, and changes in the economy, but no single measure captures every aspect of wellbeing.

## Core aggregates

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country during a specified period. **Gross national product (GNP)** adjusts domestic production for net factor income from abroad. A gross measure includes depreciation; a net measure subtracts depreciation.

The exact relationship depends on the convention used, but the key exam habit is to state whether the measure is domestic or national and whether it is gross or net before applying a formula.

## Three approaches

The **product or output approach** adds the value of final goods and services or the value added at each stage. The **income approach** adds factor incomes such as wages, rent, interest, and profit, with the relevant adjustments. The **expenditure approach** adds spending on final output, commonly represented as consumption, investment, government expenditure, and net exports.

In principle, the three approaches measure the same flow from different angles.

## Nominal and real measures

Nominal GDP values output at current prices. Real GDP values output using constant prices, making it more useful for comparing changes in physical production over time. A rise in nominal GDP may therefore reflect higher prices, higher output, or both.

## Avoiding double counting

Intermediate goods are used to produce other goods, while final goods are purchased for final use. Adding the full value of intermediate and final goods together would count the same output more than once. The value-added method avoids this problem by adding the increase in value at each stage of production.

## Measuring change carefully

Per-capita measures divide an aggregate by population, but averages can hide inequality. National income statistics are useful evidence, yet they should be interpreted with attention to informal activity, unpaid work, environmental costs, and distribution.

## Practice prompt

Explain why a new machine purchased by a firm can appear as investment expenditure even though it is not a consumer good. Then state one reason why real GDP is more useful than nominal GDP for comparing production across years.
