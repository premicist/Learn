## 1. What is a Production Possibility Curve?

Imagine an economy that can only make two things — let's say **Food** and **Clothing**. The economy has a fixed amount of resources (land, labour, capital, and technology). It cannot produce unlimited quantities of both goods. It must choose how to divide its resources between them.

A **Production Possibility Curve (PPC)** — also called a Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) — is a graph that shows **all the maximum possible combinations of two goods** an economy can produce when it uses all its resources fully and efficiently.

> **In simple words:** The PPC draws a boundary. Combinations on the boundary are the best the economy can do. Anything inside the boundary means the economy is wasting resources. Anything outside the boundary is currently impossible.

### Key Assumptions Behind the PPC

1. The economy produces **only two goods** (a simplification for illustration).
2. **Resources are fixed** — the total amount of land, labour, capital, and entrepreneurship does not change.
3. **Technology is constant** — no new inventions during the analysis.
4. **Resources are fully employed** — every worker and machine is being used.
5. **Resources are efficiently allocated** — no waste or mismanagement.

## 2. PPC Schedule (Table)

Before drawing the curve, economists first prepare a **PPC schedule** — a table listing different combinations of two goods that can be produced.

Suppose a country can produce Food (in tonnes) and Clothing (in thousands of units):

| Combination | Food (tonnes) | Clothing ('000 units) | Opportunity Cost of Clothing |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| A | 200 | 0 | — |
| B | 180 | 10 | 20 tonnes of food |
| C | 150 | 20 | 30 tonnes of food |
| D | 110 | 30 | 40 tonnes of food |
| E | 60 | 40 | 50 tonnes of food |
| F | 0 | 50 | 60 tonnes of food |

**How to read this table:**
- At combination **A**, the country puts all its resources into food and produces 200 tonnes of food with zero clothing.
- At combination **F**, it puts everything into clothing and produces 50,000 units of clothing with zero food.
- Between A and F, the country gives up some food to get more clothing.

Notice that the **opportunity cost of each additional 10,000 units of clothing keeps increasing** (20 → 30 → 40 → 50 → 60 tonnes of food). This is called **increasing opportunity cost**, and it is the reason the PPC curves outward.

## 3. Drawing the PPC from the Schedule

When we plot the combinations from the table above on a graph (Food on the Y-axis, Clothing on the X-axis) and connect the points, we get a **concave (bowed-out) curve**:

![PPC curve derived from schedule](/images/uploads/ppc-from-schedule.svg)

- The **Y-axis** shows Food production.
- The **X-axis** shows Clothing production.
- Points A through F are plotted and joined with a smooth curve.
- The curve bows outward from the origin because of increasing opportunity cost.

## 4. Points on, inside, and outside the PPC

The PPC divides the production space into three zones:

![PPC showing efficient, inefficient, and unattainable points](/images/uploads/ppc-efficiency-points.svg)

### (a) Points ON the Curve (e.g., P₁)
- The economy is using **all** its resources **efficiently**.
- It is producing the **maximum possible output** — no waste.
- To produce more of one good, it must give up some of the other.

### (b) Points INSIDE the Curve (e.g., G)
- The economy is **not using all its resources** or is using them **inefficiently**.
- There is **unemployment** or **underemployment** (idle factories, unemployed workers).
- The economy **can do better** — it can move to the curve without sacrificing anything.

### (c) Points OUTSIDE the Curve (e.g., H)
- The combination is **currently unattainable** with existing resources and technology.
- The economy **cannot reach** this point unless it gets more resources or better technology (which would shift the entire PPC outward).

## 5. Opportunity Cost and the PPC

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative that you give up when making a choice.

On the PPC:
- Moving from combination A to B means producing 10,000 more units of clothing but giving up 20 tonnes of food. So the **opportunity cost of those 10,000 clothing units is 20 tonnes of food**.
- Moving from D to E means producing 10,000 more clothing units but giving up 50 tonnes of food. The opportunity cost has **increased**.

> **Why does opportunity cost increase?** Because resources are not equally good at producing everything. When we first shift resources from food to clothing, we move the workers and land that are *least productive* in food — so we lose little food. But as we keep shifting, we move resources that are *very good* at food, causing much larger food losses for every extra unit of clothing.

The **slope of the PPC** at any point represents the opportunity cost. A steeper slope means a higher opportunity cost of clothing.

$$\text{Opportunity Cost of Clothing} = \frac{\text{Loss in Food}}{\text{Gain in Clothing}}$$

---

## 6. Why is the PPC Concave (Bowed Out)?

The PPC is **concave to the origin** (bowed outward) because of the **Law of Increasing Opportunity Cost**:

- Resources are **not perfectly adaptable** between the two goods.
- Some workers are better at farming; some are better at making clothes.
- When we transfer agricultural workers to clothing factories, food production drops more and more for each extra unit of clothing.

> **Special cases:**
> - If resources were perfectly adaptable (equally efficient at producing both goods), the PPC would be a **straight line** (constant opportunity cost).
> - If resources became *more* efficient as we specialised, the PPC would be **convex** (decreasing opportunity cost — very rare in practice).

## 7. Shifts in the PPC

The PPC is not fixed forever. It can shift **outward (rightward)** or **inward (leftward)** depending on changes in resources or technology.

![Outward and inward shifts of the PPC](/images/uploads/ppc-shifts.svg)

### (a) Outward Shift (Economic Growth)

The PPC shifts **outward** when the economy's productive capacity **increases**. This means the economy can now produce more of *both* goods. Causes include:

| Cause | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Discovery of new resources | Finding a new oil reserve or mineral deposit |
| Growth in labour force | Population growth or immigration of skilled workers |
| Technological improvement | New machinery, better farming techniques, automation |
| More capital investment | Building new factories, roads, and power plants |
| Better education and training | Workers become more skilled and productive |

### (b) Inward Shift (Economic Decline)

The PPC shifts **inward** when the economy's productive capacity **decreases**. The economy can now produce less than before. Causes include:

| Cause | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Natural disasters | Earthquake, flood, or drought destroying farmland |
| War and conflict | Destruction of factories, infrastructure, and loss of workers |
| Depletion of resources | Overuse of forests, fish stocks, or soil fertility |
| Epidemics and pandemics | Loss of productive workforce due to disease |
| Brain drain / emigration | Skilled workers leaving the country |

### (c) No Shift — Movement Along the Curve

If resources and technology stay the same but the economy simply **changes its production mix** (e.g., more food and less clothing), it **moves along the existing PPC** — no shift occurs.
